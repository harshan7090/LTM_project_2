# Inspection and Assessment Methods

## Close Interval Potential Survey (CIPS)

A CIPS measures the pipe-to-soil potential at close intervals — typically around
one metre — along the route, using a portable reference electrode connected back
to the pipe through a test post.

What it tells you: whether the cathodic protection criterion is actually met
along the whole span, and where it is not. A rectifier reading tells you what is
being delivered at the source; a CIPS tells you what is achieved at the pipe.

Surveys are normally run with the CP current interrupted on a cycle so both
"on" and "instant off" potentials are recorded. The instant-off value removes
the IR drop through the soil and is what criteria should be assessed against.

Use it when: cathodic protection is absent, suspect, or when soil conditions
raise current demand. It is inexpensive relative to excavation and it directly
tests the assumption the rest of the corrosion control strategy rests on.

## Direct Current Voltage Gradient (DCVG)

DCVG locates coating defects by measuring the voltage gradient in the soil above
the pipe while the CP current is cycled. Current flowing to a bare-steel holiday
creates a measurable gradient centred on the defect.

What it tells you: where the coating is damaged, and a relative indication of
defect severity, usually expressed as a percentage IR drop.

CIPS and DCVG are complementary and are normally run over the same span. CIPS
answers "is it protected?", DCVG answers "where is the coating broken?". A span
can pass one and fail the other, and the combination is far more informative
than either alone.

Use it especially on tape-coated pipe, where disbondment and CP shielding mean a
potential survey alone can give false assurance.

## In-line inspection (ILI)

ILI runs an instrumented tool through the pipeline with the product flow. It is
the most comprehensive assessment available for lines that can accept a tool,
because it inspects the entire length rather than sampling.

**Magnetic flux leakage (MFL)** tools magnetise the pipe wall and detect the
leakage field at metal-loss features. MFL is the workhorse for metal loss on
ferromagnetic (carbon steel) pipe. It works in gas and liquid service and
tolerates some debris.

**Ultrasonic (UT) tools** measure wall thickness directly by timing echoes.
They give more accurate depth sizing than MFL but need a liquid couplant, so
they are used in liquid lines or with a coupling slug in gas service.

**Crack-detection tools**, based on angled ultrasonics or electromagnetic
acoustic transducers, are configured specifically for planar cracking. A metal-
loss tool is not a substitute.

Piggability is the limiting factor. A line must have suitable launcher and
receiver facilities, a consistent bore, bends the tool can negotiate, and
valves with full bore. Diameter alone is a rough proxy — small-diameter lines
are often unpiggable, but a large-diameter line with mitred bends or reduced-
bore valves may be too.

## External Corrosion Direct Assessment (ECDA)

ECDA is the structured alternative for lines that cannot be inspected in-line.
The methodology is set out in NACE/AMPP SP0502 and proceeds in four steps:

1. **Pre-assessment** — gather data, define ECDA regions, and confirm the
   method is feasible for this line.
2. **Indirect inspection** — run at least two complementary above-ground
   surveys over each region, typically CIPS plus DCVG, to locate candidate
   sites.
3. **Direct examination** — excavate at prioritised sites, examine the pipe,
   and take measurements.
4. **Post-assessment** — evaluate the effectiveness of the process and set the
   reassessment interval.

The requirement for two complementary indirect techniques is deliberate: each
technique has blind spots, and using two reduces the chance a defect is missed
by both.

## Ultrasonic wall thickness measurement (UT)

Manual or automated UT measures remaining wall thickness at a specific location
using the transit time of an ultrasonic pulse. It requires access to the
surface, so on buried pipe it follows excavation.

What it gives you that surveys do not: an actual number for remaining wall.
That number is the input a fitness-for-service assessment needs. Every technique
that locates and prioritises ultimately feeds a measurement step like this one.

Limitations worth knowing: UT samples a spot, so a grid or scan pattern is
needed to characterise pitting; surface preparation matters; and heavily pitted
or rough surfaces degrade accuracy.

## Hydrostatic pressure testing

A hydrostatic test pressurises the line with water above its operating pressure.
It is a proof test: defects severe enough to fail at the test pressure do so
under controlled conditions with an incompressible fluid.

It demonstrates fitness at a point in time but gives no information about
defects that survive the test, and it requires taking the line out of service,
sourcing and disposing of water, and drying afterwards.

## Choosing between methods

A reasonable ordering logic for external corrosion threats:

- Start with the cheap indirect surveys that locate problems — CIPS for
  protection status, DCVG for coating condition.
- Use ILI where the line is piggable and quantified full-length metal loss is
  needed.
- Use ECDA where it is not.
- Follow with excavation and UT at the locations the indirect work prioritised.
- Reserve hydrostatic testing for cases where a proof test is specifically
  warranted.

The consistent principle: locate before you dig, and measure before you
calculate remaining life.
