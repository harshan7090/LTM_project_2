# External Corrosion of Buried Pipelines

## The basic electrochemical mechanism

External corrosion of buried steel pipe is an electrochemical process. It needs
four things present at once: an anode (where metal dissolves), a cathode (where
the balancing reduction reaction happens), a metallic path between them (the
pipe wall itself), and an electrolyte (the soil moisture). Remove any one and
corrosion stops — which is exactly what coatings and cathodic protection each
do, by different means.

At the anode, iron goes into solution as ferrous ions, releasing electrons.
Those electrons travel through the pipe wall to the cathodic area, where in
aerated neutral soil they are consumed by oxygen reduction, and in anaerobic or
acidic conditions by hydrogen evolution. The corrosion products that form are
the familiar iron oxides and hydroxides.

Because the pipe is a single continuous conductor, anodic and cathodic areas can
be a long way apart. A section of pipe in low-resistivity clay can act as the
anode for a section hundreds of metres away in well-drained sand. This is why
external corrosion is often concentrated at specific spans rather than spread
evenly, and why locating those spans is the first job of an inspection.

## Typical corrosion rates and what they mean

External corrosion rates on buried carbon steel are usually quoted in mils per
year (mpy), where one mil is one thousandth of an inch (0.0254 mm).

As a rough orientation: well-coated pipe with functioning cathodic protection
often corrodes at a fraction of a mil per year. Bare or badly coated pipe in
aggressive soil without protection can reach 10-20 mpy. Rates above roughly
50 mpy are exceptional in soil-side service and usually indicate something
specific — a shielded coating disbondment, stray current interference, or
microbiologically influenced corrosion — rather than ordinary uniform attack.

A predicted rate is not a measurement. Converting a predicted rate into a
remaining-life figure requires a measured wall thickness from ultrasonic testing
or in-line inspection, plus a defined limiting thickness. Without a measurement,
a rate only tells you where to look first.

## Factors that drive the rate

**Soil resistivity** is the strongest single environmental factor. It measures
how easily current flows through the soil, and therefore how easily corrosion
currents flow. Lower resistivity means more corrosive. Values are commonly
grouped roughly as: below about 1,000 ohm-cm is very corrosive, 1,000-5,000
ohm-cm is moderately corrosive, and above about 10,000 ohm-cm is relatively
benign. Resistivity varies with moisture content, so a single dry-season reading
can be misleading.

**Coating condition** determines how much steel is actually exposed. An intact
coating is a barrier; corrosion can only occur at defects, called holidays.

**Cathodic protection** shifts the pipe's potential so the whole surface becomes
cathodic, suppressing the anodic dissolution reaction.

**Age** matters mostly as a proxy for accumulated coating degradation and total
exposure time, not because steel becomes intrinsically more reactive.

**Temperature** raises reaction rates generally, but its most important effect
on buried pipe is on coating adhesion and on whether insulation stays damp.

**Dissolved salts, chlorides and sulphates** lower resistivity and can break
down protective films locally, promoting pitting rather than uniform loss.

## Uniform versus localised attack

Uniform (general) corrosion removes metal at a broadly even rate across a
surface. It is the easiest form to predict and the least likely to cause a
sudden failure, because wall loss is gradual and detectable.

Localised corrosion concentrates metal loss into small areas. Pitting can
penetrate a wall while the surrounding metal remains essentially full
thickness, so average wall-loss figures badly understate the risk. For integrity
purposes localised attack is far more dangerous than uniform attack at the same
average rate, and inspection methods have to be chosen with that in mind — a
technique that averages over an area can miss a pit entirely.
