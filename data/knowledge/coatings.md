# Pipeline Coatings

## The role of a coating

A coating is the primary barrier against soil-side corrosion. Cathodic
protection is the secondary defence, sized to handle the small area of steel
exposed at coating defects. A coating that fails wholesale does not simply
remove the barrier — it also overwhelms the CP system that was designed on the
assumption the barrier was mostly intact.

Coating defects are called holidays. Locating them without excavating is the
purpose of above-ground indirect survey techniques such as DCVG.

## Common coating types and how they age

**Fusion Bonded Epoxy (FBE)** is a thin-film thermoset applied to preheated
pipe. It bonds tightly to the steel, resists disbondment well, and — critically
— when it does fail it tends to fail in a way that still allows cathodic
protection current to reach the steel. It is generally regarded as the best
performer of the widely used options for buried service.

**Coal tar enamel** is a traditional hot-applied coating with a long service
record. It performs well for decades but becomes brittle with age and can crack
and disbond, particularly where the pipe has been subject to soil movement or
where operating temperatures are elevated. On older pipe it is often at or past
its expected service life.

**Polyethylene tape** is applied as a wrapped film. Its significant weakness is
disbondment: the tape can lift from the steel surface, especially at the
overlap spiral and at bends. The resulting gap collects moisture and, because
polyethylene is an electrical insulator, it can shield cathodic protection
current from reaching the corroding surface.

**Uncoated (bare) pipe** has no barrier at all. Every square metre of the
surface is a potential corrosion site and the entire CP current demand falls on
the protection system. Bare pipe in low-resistivity soil without CP represents
the highest-risk configuration in ordinary buried service.

## CP shielding: why disbonded tape is especially dangerous

CP shielding deserves separate emphasis because it defeats the usual assurance
process.

When a non-shielding coating fails, bare steel is exposed, cathodic protection
current reaches it, and a potential survey shows the current demand. The system
works as designed and the survey sees the problem.

When a shielding coating such as polyethylene tape disbonds, an electrolyte-
filled gap forms between the tape and the steel. The tape blocks CP current from
entering that gap. Corrosion proceeds underneath, sometimes aggressively, while
the above-ground potential survey reads a perfectly acceptable value because
the shielded area is electrically isolated from the measurement.

The practical consequence: **on tape-coated pipe, a passing close interval
potential survey does not by itself demonstrate the pipe is protected.** Coating
condition has to be assessed separately, which is why DCVG or an equivalent
coating survey is paired with a potential survey in direct assessment
methodology.

## Coating condition and inspection planning

Coating type should directly influence which inspection technique is chosen:

- Uncoated pipe: attack is distributed, so a coating survey adds little.
  Priority is confirming CP adequacy and quantifying metal loss.
- Tape coating: coating survey is essential, and potential-survey results must
  be interpreted with shielding in mind.
- Ageing coal tar enamel: coating condition survey establishes remaining life
  of the barrier before deciding on recoating versus increased CP.
- FBE in good condition: routine monitoring is usually adequate absent other
  indicators.
