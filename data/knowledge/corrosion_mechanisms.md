# Localised Corrosion Mechanisms

## Pitting corrosion

Pitting is localised attack that produces cavities of small surface area but
potentially great depth. It is dangerous out of proportion to the metal it
removes, because a pit can perforate a wall while the surrounding steel remains
essentially full thickness.

Pits initiate where a protective film breaks down locally — at an inclusion, a
scratch, a deposit, or a chloride-rich spot. Once started, a pit tends to be
self-sustaining: the pit interior becomes acidic and depleted of oxygen relative
to the surrounding surface, which maintains the potential difference driving
the attack.

Visually, pitting appears as discrete cavities or clusters of cavities, often
under corrosion product or a deposit. Because the mouth of a pit can be small
relative to its depth, and can be covered by a cap of corrosion product, **pit
depth cannot be judged reliably from a photograph.** Depth requires ultrasonic
measurement, pit gauge measurement after cleaning, or in-line inspection data.

## Crevice corrosion

Crevice corrosion occurs in shielded, stagnant gaps — under gaskets, at pipe
supports and saddles, beneath deposits, under disbonded coating, and at
lap joints. The gap holds a small volume of electrolyte that cannot exchange
freely with the bulk environment. Oxygen inside is consumed and not replenished,
the chemistry inside becomes aggressive, and attack concentrates there.

On buried and above-ground pipe, contact points with supports are a classic
location. They are also easy to overlook because they are physically obscured
by the very thing causing the problem.

## Galvanic corrosion

Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are in electrical contact
in a shared electrolyte. The more active metal becomes the anode and corrodes
preferentially, and the rate depends strongly on the ratio of cathode area to
anode area. A small anode connected to a large cathode corrodes very fast.

On pipelines this shows up at fittings, valves and repair clamps made of
different alloys, and where new pipe is tied into old. Isolation joints are
used specifically to break this electrical continuity.

## Microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC)

MIC is corrosion driven or accelerated by the metabolic activity of
microorganisms. The most commonly implicated group on buried pipe is
sulphate-reducing bacteria (SRB), which thrive in anaerobic conditions — under
deposits, under disbonded coating, and in waterlogged, poorly drained soil.

SRB reduce sulphate to sulphide, producing iron sulphide corrosion products.
Characteristic field indicators include black corrosion product, a hydrogen
sulphide smell when the deposit is disturbed, and pitting with a distinctive
morphology often described as hemispherical pits or cup-shaped depressions,
sometimes with pits inside pits.

MIC is important because it can produce very high localised rates that a model
based on soil resistivity and coating alone will underestimate, and because
cathodic protection criteria developed for abiotic corrosion may not be
sufficient where SRB are active.

## Stress corrosion cracking (SCC)

SCC produces cracks rather than metal loss, requiring the simultaneous presence
of a susceptible material, a specific environment, and tensile stress. On buried
pipelines it is typically associated with particular coating types that disbond
and trap a specific electrolyte chemistry against the steel.

SCC matters here for one methodological reason: **inspection techniques that
find metal loss do not necessarily find cracks.** A magnetic flux leakage tool
configured for metal loss will not reliably detect axially-oriented cracking.
If SCC is a credible threat, the assessment method has to be chosen for it
specifically.

## Corrosion under insulation (CUI)

CUI affects insulated pipe where water penetrates the insulation and is held
against the pipe surface. The insulation both admits water — through damaged
cladding, at seams and at penetrations — and prevents it from drying.

CUI is most severe in a temperature band where the surface stays wet rather than
either freezing or drying out; roughly the range spanning ambient to moderately
elevated temperatures. Lines that cycle in and out of this band, or that operate
intermittently, are especially exposed.

The defining practical difficulty is that CUI is invisible from outside. The
insulation and cladding look intact while significant wall loss develops
underneath. Detection requires either removing insulation at selected locations
or using a technique that can see through it, such as profile radiography.
