# Cathodic Protection

## What cathodic protection does

Cathodic protection (CP) suppresses corrosion by making the entire pipe surface
cathodic. Current is forced onto the structure from an external source, so the
anodic dissolution reaction that consumes the pipe wall is driven towards
zero and the balancing reaction happens on a sacrificial anode or an impressed
current groundbed instead.

CP does not repair existing metal loss and it does not replace a coating. It is
designed to protect the small fraction of steel exposed at coating defects. On
bare or heavily degraded pipe the current demand becomes very large, which is
why CP and coatings are treated as a combined system rather than alternatives.

## The −850 mV criterion

The most widely applied criterion for protection of buried steel is a pipe-to-
soil potential of −850 mV or more negative, measured against a saturated
copper/copper sulphate reference electrode (CSE). This criterion, along with
alternatives such as a 100 mV cathodic polarisation shift, is set out in
NACE/AMPP SP0169, the standard practice for control of external corrosion on
buried or submerged metallic piping systems.

An important practical distinction is between an "on" potential, measured with
the CP current still flowing, and an "instant off" potential, measured in the
brief interval after interrupting the current. The "on" reading includes an IR
drop through the soil, which makes protection look better than it is. Criteria
are properly assessed against the polarised, IR-free potential.

Over-protection is also a real concern. Potentials substantially more negative
than required can generate hydrogen at the steel surface, which can disbond
coatings and, on susceptible high-strength materials, contribute to hydrogen
embrittlement.

## Systems: galvanic versus impressed current

**Galvanic (sacrificial) anode systems** use a more active metal — magnesium,
zinc or aluminium alloys — connected to the pipe. The anode corrodes
preferentially. These systems are simple, need no power supply, and deliver
limited current, which suits well-coated pipe in low-resistivity soil.

**Impressed current systems** use a rectifier to drive current from an inert or
semi-inert groundbed. They deliver far more current and can protect long runs
of poorly coated pipe, but they need a power supply, regular monitoring, and
careful design to avoid interfering with neighbouring structures.

## Why a rectifier reading is not proof of protection

A common and consequential mistake is to treat a healthy rectifier output as
evidence that the pipe is protected. The rectifier tells you current is being
delivered somewhere. It does not tell you the potential achieved at any
particular point along the line.

Protection can fail locally while the rectifier reads normally because of:

- **Coating disbondment with shielding.** Some coatings, notably polyethylene
  tape, can lift from the steel and block CP current from reaching the metal
  underneath while still preventing the survey from seeing bare steel.
  Corrosion proceeds in the shielded gap.
- **Attenuation.** Current density falls with distance from the drain point, so
  the far end of a long span may be under-protected.
- **Stray current interference** from neighbouring structures, DC transit
  systems or other CP systems, which can force current off the pipe at
  specific locations, creating an intense local anode.
- **Shorted casings or contact with other buried metalwork**, which drains
  current away from the section that needs it.

This is why close interval potential surveys exist: they measure the potential
along the pipe at short intervals rather than inferring it from the source.

## Interpreting an absent-CP finding

If records show cathodic protection is absent on a segment, that is one of the
most actionable findings in an integrity dataset. Confirming it is inexpensive
relative to excavation, and the result changes the entire mitigation plan. In
combination with a degraded coating and low-resistivity soil, absent CP is the
classic high-corrosion-rate configuration.
